Research Paper

Research Project

Intelligent Demand Forecasting and Inventory Optimisation

A governed comparison of statistical and machine-learning forecasts on two retail datasets, with a hypothetical inventory simulation and a deployed research application

Youssef Saeed

Supervisor: ______________________

November 2026

Live research application: https://idf-web.vercel.app

Abstract

Retail replenishment depends on forecasts of daily demand that is noisy, seasonal and, for many products, intermittent. This project compares naive, statistical, intermittent-demand and global machine-learning forecasters on two public retail datasets under a single governed protocol, and then examines what the forecast differences would imply for inventory under explicitly assumed conditions. The Kaggle Store Item Demand dataset (500 store-item series, 913,000 daily observations, 2013 to 2017) is regular and strongly weekly-seasonal; the M5 Walmart dataset (30,490 series, 59,181,090 daily observations, 2011 to 2016) is dominated by intermittent and lumpy demand, with 68.8% zero observations in the training prefix. All full-population methods forecast 28 days ahead from four expanding validation origins (ARIMA and SARIMA only on stratified subsets), with a final 28-day holdout that was sealed, fingerprinted and evaluated exactly once after model selection was frozen.

On the final holdout the selected Store Item LightGBM model achieved a macro mean absolute error (MAE) of 5.434 units per day, 57.59% lower than seasonal naive (12.815). The selected price-free M5 random forest achieved 1.0472 against 1.0657 for a 28-day moving average (MA28), a reduction of only 1.74%, and it was worse than MA28 and the Teunter-Syntetos-Babai method on macro mean absolute scaled error: the M5 ranking depends on the metric. A deterministic lost-sales simulation of four replenishment policies over 544 cases and 8,429,280 series-episodes shows that forecast accuracy does not translate directly into lower cost. In hypothetical normalised cost units (NCU), selected-ML ordering was 376.14 NCU cheaper per series-episode than MA28 on Store Item, but the sign reversed at the last validation origin and the advantage over seasonal naive was 1.10%; on M5 it was costlier than MA28 at all four origins. These are simulated, validation-reused results, not realised savings.

The research evidence is delivered through a public Next.js application backed by a protected FastAPI simulation engine and a Supabase PostgreSQL database, whose 21 audited API responses are byte-identical to the local reference. The main contribution is methodological: a reproducible, leakage-controlled and honestly reported comparison in which the limits of the evidence are part of the result.

Keywords: demand forecasting; intermittent demand; LightGBM; random forest; rolling-origin evaluation; inventory simulation; reproducibility.

1. Introduction and problem statement

Retailers decide every day how much stock to order for thousands of store-product combinations. Ordering too little loses sales and goodwill; ordering too much ties up capital and shelf space. Both errors originate partly in the forecast of future demand. Machine-learning forecasters are widely promoted as an improvement over simple rules, but published comparisons often differ in their data, horizon, evaluation windows and error measures, which makes claims of improvement hard to interpret (Tashman, 2000; Petropoulos et al., 2022).

This project asks a narrower, testable question: under one fixed, leakage-controlled protocol, do machine-learning forecasters outperform naive and statistical baselines for 28-day daily retail forecasting, does the answer hold on a second, very different dataset, and what would the differences mean for replenishment under transparent assumptions? The datasets record sales, not demand, and contain no stock, order, lead-time or cost information. The inventory part is therefore a hypothetical simulation and is reported in normalised cost units (NCU) rather than currency.

The work was organised in governed phases: data acquisition and validation (Phase 1), training-prefix exploratory analysis and a frozen protocol (Phase 2), forecasting implementation (Phase 3), optimisation and a one-time final evaluation (Phase 4), an inventory simulation (Phase 5), a Power BI report (Phase 6) and a deployed research application (Phase 7). An independent technical audit of Phases 0 to 5 (a separate AI-assisted review, not an external human examination) returned a CONDITIONAL verdict with no critical or high findings; its reporting conditions govern how results are presented here (Section 12).

2. Research objectives and questions

The project proposal defined four research questions:

RQ1. How do naive, statistical and machine-learning approaches compare on the same daily horizons and evaluation windows?

RQ2. Which data-quality and feature choices affect forecast reliability across series?

RQ3. Does the relative ranking of methods hold on a second, independently sourced dataset?

RQ4. How sensitive are simulated service and inventory-cost trade-offs to the forecast policy, lead time and assumed operating parameters?

The corresponding objectives were to build documented ingestion, profiling and validation; to establish baselines; to perform systematic chronological tuning; to compare and interpret models; to retain reproducible evidence; and to investigate inventory implications without claiming observed savings. Causal business claims, reconstruction of actual stock and a live operational system were explicitly out of scope; the deployed application presents frozen research evidence and runs the frozen simulator on saved inputs.

3. Related work

3.1 Retail and large-scale forecasting

Fildes, Ma and Kolassa (2022) review retail forecasting research and practice, emphasising the importance of hierarchy, promotions, intermittency and the gap between accuracy and decision value. The M5 competition, built on Walmart unit sales, provides the second dataset used here; Makridakis, Spiliotis and Assimakopoulos (2022a) describe its design and data, and the results paper (2022b) reports that machine-learning methods, notably LightGBM-based approaches, were prominent among the most accurate submissions. The present study does not reproduce the competition: it uses a common 28-day leaf-level task, its own windows and no official weighted scaled error.

3.2 Statistical and intermittent-demand methods

Exponential smoothing with trend and seasonality originates in the work of Holt (2004, reprinting a 1957 memorandum) and Winters (1960); Hyndman et al. (2002) placed these methods in a state-space framework. For intermittent demand, Croston (1972) proposed separate smoothing of demand sizes and inter-demand intervals; Syntetos and Boylan (2001) showed Croston's estimator to be biased, and Syntetos and Boylan (2005) proposed and evaluated a bias-corrected approximation (SBA); Teunter, Syntetos and Babai (2011) proposed updating the demand probability every period (TSB), which also handles obsolescence. Syntetos, Boylan and Croston (2005) introduced the average inter-demand interval (ADI) and squared coefficient of variation (CV²) categorisation with cut-offs of 1.32 and 0.49, used here descriptively.

3.3 Machine learning and global models

Random forests (Breiman, 2001) and gradient-boosted trees such as LightGBM (Ke et al., 2017) can learn non-linear relationships between lagged sales, calendar features and identifiers. Januschowski et al. (2020) distinguish local models, fitted per series, from global models fitted across series; Montero-Manso and Hyndman (2021) analyse why global models can perform well on groups of series. This project fits one global model per dataset and forecasts recursively, which exposes the model to its own errors over the 28-day horizon.

3.4 Evaluation methodology

Tashman (2000) reviews out-of-sample and rolling-origin evaluation; Bergmeir and Benítez (2012) examine cross-validation for time-series predictors; Hyndman and Athanasopoulos (2021) describe chronological cross-validation and simple benchmarks. Hyndman and Koehler (2006) proposed the mean absolute scaled error (MASE), which divides errors by an in-sample naive error and is defined across series with different scales. Scale-dependent and scaled measures can rank methods differently, a result observed on M5 in this project.

3.5 Censored demand

Sales data understate demand when stock-outs occur, because unmet demand is not recorded (Conrad, 1976). Because neither dataset records stock, this project cannot separate low demand from unavailability; it treats sales as a demand proxy and states this as a threat to validity.

6. Forecasting methodology

6.1 Task and horizon

For every series, each method predicts expected daily unit sales for the next 28 calendar days from a forecast origin, without using any actual sales inside the forecast window. Four complete weeks give each weekday equal exposure. Forecasts are not rounded, are clipped below at zero, and every model must deliver a forecast for every series; a predeclared seasonal-naive fallback (zero for an all-zero history) covers fitting failures and is counted. No fallback was needed in the final assessment.

6.2 Models

Baselines were the zero forecast, naive (last value), weekly seasonal naive, 28-day moving average (MA28) and a seasonal moving average. Statistical comparators were simple exponential smoothing, Holt, additive Holt-Winters, ARIMA(1,0,0) and SARIMA(1,0,0)(1,0,0)7 on a reproducible stratified subset only, and the intermittent methods Croston, SBA and TSB. Machine-learning comparators were a global random forest (scikit-learn; Pedregosa et al., 2011) and a global LightGBM regressor (Ke et al., 2017), each fitted separately per dataset as a one-step model and applied recursively for 28 steps.

6.3 Features and information availability

Every feature for a label at date t uses only observations strictly before t; during recursion, lag and rolling features are recomputed from the model's own clipped predictions, while occurrence statistics are frozen at the origin so that predictions are never treated as observed sales. 7.1 Metrics and aggregation

With error e = forecast minus actual, the project reports MAE, RMSE, WAPE, signed bias, MASE and RMSSE (definitions in Appendix A, Table 15). Macro measures average per-series values, giving each series equal weight; pooled measures aggregate all errors first. MASE and RMSSE are scaled by the in-sample weekly seasonal-naive error of each series (Hyndman and Koehler, 2006). Undefined values, such as WAPE for a series with no sales in the window or a scaled error with a zero scale, are kept undefined and counted, never replaced by zero or an epsilon. The leaf-level weekly RMSSE is not the official hierarchical M5 WRMSSE.

7.2 Selection and the sealed final holdout

The primary selection objective, declared before scoring, was the mean over the four validation origins of macro MAE, separately per dataset. Phase 4 evaluated 27 configurations, of which 23 were seed-42 random-search tuning trials (12 Store Item: six LightGBM, three random forest and three Holt-Winters; 11 M5: four random forest, three LightGBM and four TSB) and four were unchanged references, giving 108 fold runs and 45,177,440 validation forecasts, all independently reconciled. ML training used outcome-independent stratified samples of 300 labels per Store Item series and six per M5 series per origin.

The selection was then frozen in a manifest whose SHA-256 fingerprint (a71ab187…) covers the chosen models, features, comparators and 89 source and configuration files. All eight final forecast bundles (3,470,880 forecasts) were sealed before the first holdout read, and each dataset's holdout was read once, as recorded in a 12-event hash-chained access ledger. The final result was not used to change any selection.

9. Inventory simulation

9.1 Design and assumptions

The simulator is a discrete, periodic-review, lost-sales model run on the saved validation sales of each series and origin, using the fixed-origin validation forecasts (never refreshed during an episode and never using final-holdout demand). Forecast policies share an order-up-to rule: at each review the order is the positive part of the forecast over the lead time plus review period, plus a buffer, minus the inventory position, rounded up to whole units. A fixed reorder-point comparator orders a fixed quantity when the inventory position falls to a threshold. Base assumptions were frozen before any result: lead time 7 days, review every day, buffer 3 days of the trailing 56-day mean, initial stock 7 days of that mean, holding 1 NCU per unit per day, unmet demand 10 NCU per unit and 1 NCU per order. The formulae are in Appendix B.

Every one of the 544 cases (two datasets, four origins, four policies, 17 scenarios) completed on the full populations, giving 8,429,280 series-episodes; the project's own scalar replay of 3,264 episodes confirmed the arithmetic, the independent audit's exact-integer re-simulation of the three deterministic policies found only negligible ceiling-boundary differences, and the final audit re-aggregated all base results from the raw per-series outputs with zero discrepancy.

Three findings follow. First, accuracy and cost diverge: on Store Item the ML forecast is more accurate than MA28 at every origin, yet costlier at origin 4, where 473 of 500 series cost more; cost depends on the forecast level relative to the order-up-to target, not on error magnitude alone. Why can a more accurate forecast cost more? Under an order-up-to policy the order quantity is driven by the level of the summed forecast over the protection interval (eight days in the base case), and holding cost accrues on every unit of excess stock, whereas a shortage is incurred only when stock runs out. A forecast whose absolute error is smaller but whose level is biased upwards in a particular window therefore builds more stock than a lower, less accurate forecast. The asymmetric cost ratio (10 NCU per unmet unit against 1 NCU per unit-day held) and the identical days-of-cover buffer for all policies mean that small level differences multiply into cost differences that need not follow the ranking by MAE.

9.3 The fixed reorder-point comparator

The fixed reorder-point rule has the lowest cost in both datasets, but this reflects lower stock, not better management (Table 11). On Store Item it orders at essentially every review because its inventory position (7.33 mean days of demand) never rises above the 11-day reorder point, so it behaves as a daily one-day replenishment; on M5 it acts as a threshold rule. Its fill rate is lower than the ML policy in 15 of 17 Store Item scenarios and all 17 M5 scenarios. It is therefore reported as a low-stock, lower-service comparator.

10. System architecture and implementation

The research pipeline is a Python package (data adapters, exploratory analysis, forecasting, evaluation, inventory, preservation and reporting) with a command-line interface, configuration files and 352 automated tests at the Phase 4 checkpoint. Phase 6 delivered a five-page Power BI report (86 visuals, 69 DAX measures) whose 43-rule quality check (42 pass, 1 cosmetic warning) reconciles the report's measures with the saved evidence. Phase 7 delivered the public research application (Figure 10).

Figure 10. Deployed architecture. The browser holds no credentials; demand and forecasts for simulations are loaded server-side.

The Next.js application serves five pages (Executive Overview, Forecast Explorer, Inventory Planner, Scenario Comparison, Research and Methodology) from eleven versioned API routes. A FastAPI service wraps the byte-identical frozen Phase 5 engine and is reachable only with a shared key and Vercel's protection bypass. Supabase PostgreSQL holds 655,711 exported evidence rows behind two read-only roles. Validation, rate limiting, generic errors and security headers protect the public interface. Two defects found during deployment are instructive. First, Supabase rounds text-transferred double-precision values to 15 significant digits, which changed integer inventory results for 182 of 3,200 series-origin pairs; the engine now reads binary floats and the web API reads inside transactions that request exact output. Second, a TLS option in the database connection string was forwarded to the server as a startup parameter and rejected; it is now applied on the client. Both were found only because exact parity with the local reference was required.

11. Discussion

The contrast between the datasets is the central result. Store Item series are regular, so lags and calendar effects carry real signal and a global gradient-boosting model can exploit them; the 57.59% final improvement over seasonal naive is large and consistent across origins. M5 leaf series are mostly intermittent: two thirds of the sampled training labels are zero, and a 28-day mean is already close to the best attainable point forecast for many series. In that regime the random forest's advantage is small, concentrated outside the sparsest series, and not robust to the choice of scaled metric. This is consistent with the literature's view that the value of complex models depends on the data (Petropoulos et al., 2022).

The inventory study shows why accuracy alone is an incomplete criterion. An order-up-to policy is sensitive to the forecast level: a forecast with lower absolute error but a systematically higher level raises stock and holding cost. Better forecasts did not reliably lower simulated cost, and the cheapest policy was the one that held the least stock and served the fewest units. Any operational recommendation would require service targets, real costs and a fresh evaluation window, none of which these datasets provide.

For practice, the results suggest a pragmatic sequence: for regular, high-volume items a global gradient-boosting model was clearly more accurate on the one regular dataset studied, although its cost advantage was not robust; for intermittent items a well-chosen simple method such as a 28-day mean or TSB remains a strong default, and model choice should be validated on the decision metric (cost and service) rather than on accuracy alone. They also show the value of reporting results per evaluation window: a single pooled figure would have hidden the Store Item reversal at origin 4.

The governance design is a contribution in its own right. The selection fingerprint, sealed forecast bundles and once-only holdout ledger make it possible to demonstrate, rather than assert, that the final result did not influence model choice. The same discipline carried into deployment: the public application serves byte-identical evidence and runs the byte-identical frozen engine, and the two defects found in production, one of them a silent loss of floating-point precision, were detected only because exact, not approximate, parity was required.

Regarding RQ2, the decisive data choices were the exclusion of prices and events whose availability at the forecast origin could not be verified, the retention of zeros and missing values instead of imputation, and full-population evaluation. Price-enabled Phase 3 experiments are kept only as conditional references, because using prices that might not have been published by the origin would risk leakage.

13. Conclusions and future work

Under one governed protocol, global machine learning gave a large forecasting improvement on regular retail demand and only a marginal, metric-dependent improvement on intermittent demand. Forecast accuracy did not translate reliably into lower simulated inventory cost. The project's contribution is a reproducible, leakage-controlled and transparently reported comparison, with a sealed final evaluation, full-population results on 30,490 M5 series, an audited simulation and a deployed application that serves exactly the recorded evidence for its curated series (all 500 Store Item and 300 of 30,490 M5 series).

Future work should (i) evaluate on a fresh, untouched window to remove the selection optimism of the inventory study; (ii) add a properly tuned (s, S) comparator and service-level targets; (iii) model censored demand explicitly; (iv) test price and event features under verified publication-availability assumptions; (v) report distributional forecasts for safety-stock decisions; and (vi) repeat the analysis on a clean machine to confirm reproduction from source.

Appendix B. Inventory policy mathematics

Let μ be the trailing 56-day training mean, L the lead time, R the review interval, P = L + R, B = b·μ the buffer and IP = closing stock + pipeline. Orders placed at the end of day t arrive at the start of day t + L + 1; unmet demand is lost.

Forecast policies:  S = (f(t+1) + f(t+2) + … + f(t+P)) + B,   order = ⌈ max(0, S − IP) ⌉

Fixed reorder point:  s = μ·P + B,   Q = ⌈ μ·R ⌉,   order Q if IP ≤ s

Daily cost = h·closing + p·unmet + k·1[order > 0] + v·ordered;  terminal e·(closing + pipeline)

Base parameters: L = 7, R = 1, b = 3, initial stock 7·μ, h = 1, p = 10, k = 1, v = 0, e = 0 NCU. Pooled fill rate = Σ fulfilled / Σ demand. Average on-hand is the mean daily closing stock.
